# Living in France and working in Geneva: how to count telework and mission days

**Short answer:** If you live in France and work for an employer in Geneva, telework from France stays taxable in Switzerland up to **40% of your annual working time** in a calendar year. That 40% can include **up to 10 days of temporary missions** in France or another country. Above 40%, the pay for your telework days becomes taxable in France **from the first telework day**, not just the excess.

Social security is a **separate counter**: from 25% telework in France, French social security normally applies, unless your employer obtains an A1 under the framework agreement, which is available only below 50%.

This is a practical record-keeping explainer based on official public sources checked on 10 November 2026 (Europe/Zurich). It is **not tax or legal advice** and does not guarantee compliance. Check with your employer, a qualified adviser or the competent authority. A personal log does not replace the telework rate your employer reports to the tax office.

## 1. Which rule this is, and which it is not

The 40% rule comes from the amendment of 27 June 2023 to the France-Switzerland tax treaty of 9 September 1966. The amendment **entered into force on 24 July 2025** and **applies from 1 January 2026**. The 40% limit itself is not new: until 31 December 2025, France and Switzerland applied the same limits under a transitional amicable agreement. What changed in 2026 is the treaty basis and the employer's duty to track the days worked.

The Geneva cantonal tax office (AFC) sets out the regime on ge.ch for people who live in France and work in Switzerland while doing part of their work by telework from France. The Swiss Federal Tax Administration (ESTV) states that the amendment's telework rules apply throughout Switzerland, with Swiss nationals working for public-law employers excluded (subject to treaty Art. 21(2)). This guide follows the Geneva AFC's published practice; if your employer is in another canton outside the 1983 agreement, the same treaty limits apply, but check that canton's reporting forms.

This is **not** the cross-border worker agreement of 11 April 1983. That agreement covers the cantons of Bern, Solothurn, Basel-Stadt, Basel-Landschaft, Vaud, Valais, Neuchâtel and Jura, and has its own extra rules, including a limit on nights away from home. If you work in Geneva, do not size your telework with that 45-night limit.

Nationality and the public sector can change the answer. In its FAQ on telework above 40%, the Geneva AFC answers for a person **with Swiss nationality**: in the private sector, telework days in France are taxable in France; in the public sector they generally stay taxable in Switzerland, unless the employer carries on an industrial and commercial activity (treaty Art. 21(2)). Public-sector pay falls under a separate treaty article and is outside the scope of this guide.

## 2. The tax counter: 40%, including up to 10 mission days

### What counts as telework

The treaty protocol defines telework as work that could also have been done at the employer's premises, done by the employee in their state of residence, remotely and outside the employer's premises, on the employer's behalf, under the employment contract, using information and communication technology.

ge.ch adds that the telework must be done **from France**, your country of residence. That can be your main home, but also a second home, a hotel or a co-working space, as long as it is in France.

- **Sick days and holidays are not telework**: no work is done on those days.
- **On-call periods** count as telework **only if you actually intervene** in France. Without an intervention they do not count. The amicable agreement of 29 April 2026 adds that on-call time is not, in itself, a temporary mission.

### Temporary missions are logged separately from ordinary telework

A temporary mission, per ge.ch, is a workday outside the employer's premises **that is not telework**, done in France or in another country: a client or supplier visit, an external meeting, training, and so on.

Mission days count inside the 40% **only within a double limit**: 10 days a year and 40% of annual working time, **after** ordinary telework days have been counted. This is set out in the France-Switzerland amicable agreement of 29 April 2026 and in the French tax office (DGFiP) fact sheet, which explicitly covers the regime outside the 1983 agreement and uses examples of an employee living in Annemasse and working for an employer in Geneva.

The official counting order:

1. Ordinary telework days (that are not missions) are counted first.
2. Among missions, those **in France** are counted before those **in a third country**.
3. Any mission days that exceed either the 10 days or what is left under 40% **are not** treated as telework. Per the DGFiP fact sheet, pay for those excess days is taxed under the ordinary treaty rules (in France in its examples), without prejudice to the treaty between France and the third country concerned.

If you work **part-time for the whole year**, or work **only part of the year**, the 10-day limit is reduced in proportion and **rounded up** to a whole day.

Travel time to a mission, per the ge.ch table: it does not count as a mission if no work is done, or if the work that day is done mostly in Switzerland. It counts if the work is done mostly abroad.

### If you go above 40%

Per ge.ch, if you telework from France for **more than 40%** of your working time in a calendar year, the part of your salary for the telework days is taxable in France **from day one**. The part for days worked in Switzerland stays taxable in Switzerland. ge.ch adds that above 40%, all temporary mission days are taxable in France too.

There is **no weekly limit**: only the annual 40% has to be respected, and the weekly pattern is free as long as the annual cap is not exceeded. The AFC says there is **no exception** to the 40% limit, not even for health reasons.

For part-time work, the rule applies in proportion to your activity rate. ge.ch's example: full-time, 40% is about 2 days a week; at 50%, about 1 day a week. That illustrates a rhythm, not a separate weekly cap.

On a simplified base of **240 working days** for full-time work, 40% is **96 days**. ge.ch and the DGFiP fact sheet use this base in their examples. It is not a fixed legal cap of 96 days for everyone: the number depends on the working time actually used (part-time, part of a year, or a base other than 240 days).

## 3. How your Geneva employer counts, and why your log should match

The treaty amendment does not set a method for calculating the rate; it only sets the 40% limit. ge.ch leaves the method to the employer (days, half days, actual hours and so on), **provided the same method is applied to all staff**, without exception.

The AFC example for an employer counting in days:

| Method | Rule | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Whole days (8 hours) | A telework day counts if telework lasts **more than 4 hours** | 5 hours = 1 telework day |
| Half days (4 hours) | A half day counts if telework lasts **2 to 4 hours**; **nothing under 2 hours** | 3 hours = half a day |
| Actual hours | Every hour of telework counts | 3 hours = 3 hours |

Total working days can be tracked individually, or on a simplified base of 240 days a year for full-time work.

**Ask your employer which method it uses.** A log kept in half days will not read the same as an hourly count.

Since **1 January 2026**, a Geneva employer must track the telework and temporary mission days **actually worked** by each employee living in France, to calculate the rate it reports to the AFC at the start of the following year. The **first report is due in January 2027 for 2026**. What is reported is a **rate** (a percentage), not a number of days, and it includes at most 10 mission days.

## 4. The social security counter: a different rule and different percentages

The 40% tax rule is not social security. The Swiss Federal Social Insurance Office (FSIO / BSV), in a page published on 12 September 2025, states that its notice "concerns only social insurance, not tax law".

France and Switzerland have both applied the framework agreement on habitual cross-border telework since **1 July 2023**. On a joint request from employer and employee, it lets an employee stay insured in the employer's state if cross-border telework in the state of residence is **less than 50%** of total working time.

- **Under 25%:** the framework agreement does not apply and the ordinary rules and procedures continue to apply.
- **From 25% to under 50%:** under the general EU rule (Article 13 of Regulation (EC) No 883/2004), you are normally insured in France, your state of residence, if you do at least 25% of your working time or pay there. The framework agreement can keep you in Swiss social security for telework between 25% and 49.9% of working time, as the FSIO puts it.
- **50% or more:** the framework agreement is not available.

The FSIO lists situations the agreement does **not** cover, including: another habitual activity in the state of residence besides telework (for example regular client visits, or a self-employed side activity); a habitual activity in another EU/EFTA state besides the state of residence and Switzerland; another employer in the EU/EFTA; and self-employed people.

For the agreement to apply, the Swiss employer must request an **A1 certificate** (valid for up to 3 years, renewable) from its AHV/AVS compensation office through the ALPS platform. For requests filed from 1 July 2024, an A1 can be backdated by at most three months.

So you can stay under 40% for tax and already be in the 25% to under 50% social security band. From 25%, French social security normally applies, unless you stay in Switzerland under the framework agreement on request and with an A1. Between 40% and under 50%, you are over the tax cap but still inside the framework agreement band. These are two different readings of the same calendar, and their calculation bases can differ.

## 5. Worked example (240-day base)

These three cases are taken from the AFC's published examples.

| Case | Telework in France | Missions (France or elsewhere) | Days in Switzerland | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 72 days (30%) | 0 | 168 | All 240 days taxable in Switzerland. Rate reported: 30.00% |
| B | 91 days (38%) | 8 days | 141 | 40% (96 days) is reached with 91 telework days plus 5 mission days. The other **3 mission days** are taxable in France. Rate reported: 40.00% |
| C | 144 days (60%) | 5 days | 91 | Telework alone is above 40%, so the telework regime does not apply: **all 144 telework days and all 5 mission days** are taxable in France. Rate reported: 60.00%, without mission days |

Case B shows why missions must be logged apart from telework: the same 8 mission days end up split between Switzerland and France.

## 6. Checklist for employees

1. **Separate three kinds of workdays:** on site in Geneva; telework in France; temporary mission (France or another country). Also log non-working days (leave, sickness, public holiday not worked), which do not feed the telework rate.
2. **Do not mix missions with ordinary telework.** Note whether each mission was in France or in another country: the order of counting depends on it. On the social security side, regular client visits in France are another habitual activity that can rule out the framework agreement.
3. **Match your employer's counting unit** (whole days, half days or hours). The 2-hour and 4-hour thresholds only apply if your employer counts that way.
4. **Keep a tax counter** (share of 40%, up to 10 mission days) **and a social security counter** (share of working time, 25% and 50% bands).
5. **Do not wait for year end.** Your employer already has to track the days worked in 2026. Your log can help, but your employer calculates and reports the rate.
6. **An export is not the certificate, and it does not prove where you were.** What matters is the type of day actually worked and consistency with your employer's records.

## 7. Checklist for Geneva employers

1. **Track, for each employee living in France, the telework days and temporary mission days actually worked**, from 1 January 2026.
2. **Pick one counting method** (days, half days or actual hours) and **apply it to all staff**.
3. **Report a rate, not days**, at the start of each year for the previous year, including at most 10 mission days. First report: January 2027, for 2026. **Report even when there was no telework:** the Geneva tax office says to enter 0% if there was no telework and no temporary mission outside Switzerland, and the ESTV says employers must provide the attestation even if the employee did no telework in the state of residence.
4. **Use a supported channel:** certified Swissdec ELM payroll software (version 5.3 or later), the ISeL e-service, or paper forms. The AFC says it will update ISeL and the paper forms for telework data from January 2027.
5. **Put the rate on the withholding tax summary list** (paper) or send it through ELM or ISeL. It does not have to appear on the salary certificate.
6. **Employees who leave during the year:** on request, give them the certificate for employment of less than one year (Art. 5a OIS), so the next employer knows the telework and mission days already worked. It is not sent to the AFC.
7. **Part-time staff with several employers:** report only the rate for days worked for your company. **Residence change during the year:** issue two attestation-quittances and report a rate only for the period of residence in France.
8. **Above 40%:** you may reduce the salary subject to Swiss withholding tax by the amount for telework days in France. You may not withhold tax on behalf of a foreign state without official authorisation (Article 271 of the Swiss Criminal Code).
9. **Employers in other cantons:** if you do not settle withholding tax electronically (ELM or a cantonal portal) and your canton has no form of its own, the ESTV's uniform employer attestation applies, sent to the cantonal tax office at the start of the following year. Geneva and Vaud have their own paper forms, and the uniform form is not valid there.
10. **Social security is separate:** for the framework agreement, request an A1 through ALPS.

## 8. What BorderDay can log, and what it does not do

[BorderDay](https://borderday.com/en/for-individuals/) is a tracking tool. For someone living in France and working in Geneva, the border route to pick is **"Geneva and other non-1983 cantons ← France"**, not the 1983-agreement route.

You can log four day types: "Telework at home", "On site at workplace", "Work trip / third State" and "Non-working day", plus a half day if needed. "Telework at home" is for telework done in France: the in-app hint mentions home or local co-working, and ge.ch also accepts a second home or a hotel in France. "Work trip / third State" is for missions outside Switzerland (in France or another country). There is no separate field for "mission in France" versus "mission in another country". If that distinction matters to you, keep it in your own records. Notes you add in BorderDay stay private and never appear in company exports.

There is no GPS and no device location. The tax counters (40% share and 10 mission days) and the social security counter are separate. BorderDay logs whole and half days; it has no hourly method. If your employer counts in hours, its figures prevail.

The JSON or CSV export is a working document. It does not replace the annual telework rate your employer reports to the tax office. BorderDay does not cover self-employed people, public-sector-specific rules, people with employers in several states, or work without an IT link in the sense of the framework agreement.

If your employer already uses BorderDay, join through its invitation link. Otherwise you can keep your own log from the [For individuals](https://borderday.com/en/for-individuals/) page. Information for Geneva employers is on the [Geneva employers](https://borderday.com/en/employers/geneva/) page.
