# Living in France and working in Luxembourg: how to count telework days

**Short answer:** If you are tax resident in France and employed in Luxembourg, you can spend up to **34 days per tax year** physically working in France or a third country and your whole salary stays taxable in Luxembourg. Any part of a day counts as a full day, and part-time or part-year work reduces the 34 in proportion. Above 34 days, France can tax the pay for the time you actually worked outside Luxembourg, **from the first day**.

Social security is a **separate counter** in percent of working time: the framework agreement can keep you in Luxembourg social security for telework from **25% to under 50%**, with an A1 certificate. The two thresholds do not offset each other.

This is a practical record-keeping explainer based on official public sources checked on 10 November 2026 (Europe/Zurich). It is **not tax or legal advice** and does not guarantee compliance. Check your situation with your employer, a qualified adviser or the competent authority. Rules can change, and special cases (several employers, public sector, self-employed) are outside this guide.

## 1. Two separate counters

| Counter | What it measures | Published threshold | Main consequence if exceeded |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Tax** | Days you are **physically present** in France and/or a third country **to do your job** | **34 days** per tax period, not to be exceeded ("not exceeding 34 days in total") | France can tax the pay for the time actually worked in France or a third country, **from the first day**, not just the excess (for third-country days, subject to the treaty with that country) |
| **Social security** | Share of **working time** teleworked in your country of residence | Ordinary rules under 25% and at 50% or more; **framework agreement** possible from 25% to **under 50%**, on request, with an A1 certificate | Your cover can move to your country of residence if the conditions to stay in Luxembourg are not met |

Two telework days a week from France is about 40% of working time: far above the 34 tax days. For social security, that pattern is above 25%: in principle French social security then applies, unless your employer and you obtain continued Luxembourg cover under the framework agreement (40% is under 50%), with an A1. Either way: **do not merge the two counts**.

## 2. The 34-day tax threshold: what counts and what does not

The consolidated France-Luxembourg tax treaty protocol (point 3) says that a resident of one state who works in the other state and who, during a tax period, is physically present in their state of residence and/or a third state to work there for one or more periods **not exceeding 34 days in total** is treated as working in the employer's state for the whole tax period.

The amendment of 7 November 2022 raised the threshold from 29 to 34 days. It applies to tax periods starting from 1 January 2023. France approved it by Law No. 2025-139 of 17 February 2025 and published it by Decree No. 2025-382 of 28 April 2025 (entry into force shown on Légifrance: 4 March 2025). The amendment also says the two states would meet before 31 December 2024 to set the conditions from 1 January 2025 and, if needed, sign a new amendment. That is a review clause, not an expiry date: the amendment stays in force until the end of the year after its entry into force and **is renewed if no new amendment is signed**. As of 10 November 2026, no new amendment was found, the French tax office still publishes the 34-day agreement, and the Luxembourg tax administration applies 34 days in its circular of 24 June 2026.

The France-Luxembourg amicable agreement of 16 July 2020 on how to apply the threshold (the official PDF still says "29 days", to be read as **34 days**), restated in Luxembourg circular L.G. - Conv. D.I. No. 61 of 24 June 2026, sets out the count:

**Count:**

- days you are physically present in France and/or a third country **to do your job**;
- so telework from France, but also a mission, a client visit or any other work outside Luxembourg;
- **any part of a day**, which counts as a **whole day**;
- **any day or part of a day of professional training** in France or a third country.

**Do not count**, when your presence in France or a third country is not due to your job, in particular:

- days of leave;
- weekly rest days and public holidays **when you are not required to work**;
- days of incapacity for work due to illness;
- force majeure beyond the control of employer and employee.

**Part-time or part of the year:** the threshold is reduced in proportion to the working time in the contract and/or the length of the contract. If the result is not a whole number of days, **round down**. The threshold is assessed **per year and overall**, including when you have several separate contracts in the same year. The circular's examples:

- part-time at 75%: 34 × 75% = 25.5, so **25 days**;
- contract starting 1 July: 34 × 6/12 = **17 days**;
- part-time at 75% starting 1 October: (34 × 75%) × 3/12 = 6.4, so **6 days**.

**If you exceed the threshold:** you lose the benefit of the rule for that period. Your state of residence (France, if you live there) regains the right to tax your employment pay **from the first day**, in proportion to the time you were actually present in France and/or a third country to work. For third-country days, this is subject to the treaty with that country.

A part of a day counts as a whole day when you check the threshold. Once the threshold is exceeded, though, taxation follows the time actually worked outside Luxembourg: in an example in the 2026 circular, eight mornings worked in France (afternoons in Luxembourg) are taxed in France as four days.

It is up to you to show the conditions are met and to keep relevant evidence available for the tax authorities. The amicable agreement and the 2026 circular say the most common proof asked for is evidence of your **physical presence in Luxembourg**. Examples from the non-exhaustive list: employment contract or employer certificate stating your duties and where they are performed; personal timesheets; personal transport tickets; accommodation or car rental invoices; signed attendance lists for meetings or training; receipts for purchases, meals or canteen in the country of work; personal mission orders; and any other relevant document. **A log you keep yourself, even exported, does not on its own prove where you were.**

## 3. Social security: a percentage, not 34 days

This counter is not about tax. It falls under Regulation (EC) No 883/2004 and, where relevant, the framework agreement on habitual cross-border telework, made under Article 16(1) of that Regulation (which allows exceptions to the ordinary rules of Article 13). It has applied for France and Luxembourg since **1 July 2023** (list of the Belgian FPS Social Security, the depositary).

According to Luxembourg's Joint Social Security Centre (CCSS), page last updated 24 March 2026:

- the framework agreement can apply if telework is **between 25% and less than 50%** of total professional activity, **and** all other conditions are met: **salaried** work (not self-employed); employer's state and state of residence both signatories; telework done **only** in the state of residence; **no other habitual activity** apart from the one in the employer's state; and a **connection** to the employer's IT infrastructure;
- regular telework **under 25%** or **at 50% or more** falls under the ordinary rules for work in two or more states (Article 13 of the Regulation), not under the framework agreement;
- the framework agreement is not automatic: the employer declares the telework and requests an **A1** certificate. When the framework agreement applies, the CCSS issues an A1 for the declared period, **up to 3 years**;
- since 1 July 2024, a declaration can be backdated by at most **three months**.

On the French side, CLEISS explains the general rule: if you work at least 25% of your working time or pay in your country of residence, you are in principle insured there. Below 25%, with the rest of your work at the employer's premises, the employer's state already applies.

For its own procedure, the CCSS also says that working time includes sick days but not paid holidays, and that the percentage to declare is a monthly average, declared as a whole number with the usual rounding. Its examples: one telework day a week for 12 months is 20%; two days a week is 40%. These are Luxembourg declaration rules: follow current CCSS or employer instructions rather than a home-made "34 days = X%" conversion. The official pages checked do not give a separate rule for partial days in the social security percentage.

Note the contrast: sick days are **excluded** from the 34-day tax count but **included** in working time for the CCSS percentage.

## 4. Worked example

These figures come from the Luxembourg circular of 24 June 2026 (resident of France, Luxembourg employer).

| Year | Where the work was done | Tax result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 200 days in Luxembourg, 20 days in France | 20 days, under 34: **100% taxable in Luxembourg** |
| 2 | 180 days in Luxembourg, 40 days in France | 40 days, over 34: Luxembourg taxes 180 days, **France taxes 40 days** |
| 3 | 220 planned days: 181 in Luxembourg, 25 in France, 8 days with the morning in France and the afternoon in Luxembourg, 6 training days in a third country | Count for the threshold: 25 + 8 + 6 = **39 days**, so 34 is exceeded |

In year 3, the 8 split days each count as a full day for the threshold. Once it is exceeded, those 8 mornings are taxed in France as 4 days (8 × 50%).

For social security, two telework days a week all year would be about 40%, inside the framework agreement band. Your employer's CCSS declaration, not this rough share, is what counts.

## 5. Checklist for employees

1. **Pick the right border route.** The rules in this guide come from the France-Luxembourg treaty. For a **resident of France**, the part-time and part-year reduction applies. Residents of other countries working in Luxembourg fall under other treaties: do not copy a rule written for another country of residence.
2. **Log the type of day, not a location.** At minimum: workday in Luxembourg; telework in France; trip or mission outside Luxembourg; non-working day (leave, public holiday not worked, sickness, rest).
3. **Treat any part of a day outside Luxembourg as a full tax day.** A morning of telework and an afternoon at the Luxembourg office, or an afternoon of training in France, counts as a full day for the threshold.
4. **Track the social security percentage separately.** A mission day in a third country counts toward the 34 tax days. For the social security framework agreement, telework must be done only in the state of residence, and another habitual activity outside the employer's state can take you out of the framework. A one-off trip is not automatically a "habitual activity", but a recurring pattern is worth checking with the competent institution.
5. **Keep proof of presence in Luxembourg** if you rely on the threshold. The log helps you remember; the documents help you prove.
6. **If you have several contracts in the year**, add up the days outside Luxembourg: the threshold is overall.

## 6. Checklist for Luxembourg employers

1. **Declare telework to the CCSS.** Since 1 July 2023, any telework by an employee who does not live in Luxembourg must be declared to the CCSS, whether it falls under the framework agreement (25% to under 50%) or the ordinary multi-state rules.
2. **Declare a percentage for a period, not each day:** a monthly average, as a whole number, for a period of at least 1 month and at most 3 years.
3. **Declare again when things change:** a move to another country, a change in telework time, or adding or dropping another professional activity.
4. **Renew the A1** when it expires: the employer must submit a new application.
5. **Help with tax evidence.** An employer certificate stating duties and place of work, personal timesheets and mission orders are among the documents the tax authorities list as proof of presence.

## 7. What BorderDay can log, and what it does not do

[BorderDay](https://borderday.com/en/for-individuals/) is a tracking tool. For the **Luxembourg ← France** border route, you can log four day types yourself: "Telework at home", "On site at workplace", "Work trip / third State" and "Non-working day". You can mark a half day; for this route the app reminds you that any part of a day outside Luxembourg still counts as a whole day. There is no GPS and no device location. The tax and social security counters are separate. You can export JSON or CSV.

The export is a **working document**. It does not replace a tax return, an A1 request or the documents the authorities can ask for. It does not prove your physical presence. Notes you add stay private and never appear in company exports. BorderDay does not cover self-employed people, public-sector-specific rules, people with employers in several states, or work without an IT link in the sense of the framework agreement. The counters only see the days logged in the tool.

If your employer already uses BorderDay, counters are shared through its invitation link. Otherwise you can keep your own log from the [For individuals](https://borderday.com/en/for-individuals/) page. Information for Luxembourg employers is on the [Luxembourg employers](https://borderday.com/en/employers/luxembourg/) page.
