# How to track cross-border telework days

**Short answer:** Employers of cross-border staff need a day-by-day record of where and how each employee worked, because tax and social security rules each use their own count. In Switzerland, employers of France residents (outside the 1983 cross-border agreement) must track telework and mission days actually worked and report an annual rate, even when it is 0%. In Luxembourg, every telework arrangement of a non-resident employee must be declared to social security.

Any method works if it captures the right day types, uses one counting rule for all staff, and keeps the evidence. This page compares four common approaches: spreadsheet, HR or time system, dedicated tracker, and employee self-declaration.

This is a practical overview based on official public sources checked on 10 November 2026 (Europe/Zurich). It is **not tax or legal advice**. Rules differ by border route; check with a qualified adviser or the competent authority.

## 1. Why employers need these records

**Tax, Switzerland (employees living in France, outside the 1983 agreement).** Since 1 January 2026, telework from France stays taxable in Switzerland up to 40% of annual working time, including up to 10 temporary mission days. The Geneva tax office says the employer must track the telework and temporary mission days actually worked by each employee living in France, to calculate the rate it reports at the start of the following year. The first report is due in January 2027 for 2026. The Swiss Federal Tax Administration (ESTV) says these rules apply throughout Switzerland and that employers must provide the attestation even if the employee did no telework in France.

**Tax, Luxembourg (employees living in France).** Up to 34 days a year of work physically done in France or a third country stays taxable in Luxembourg. Any part of a day counts as a whole day. It is up to the employee to prove that the conditions are met, and the documents the tax authorities list as proof include an employer certificate of duties and place of work, timesheets and mission orders.

**Social security (EU, EEA and Switzerland).** Under the general rule, an employee who does at least 25% of their working time or pay in their country of residence is in principle insured there. The framework agreement on habitual cross-border telework lets an employee stay insured in the employer's state if telework in the state of residence is under 50% of working time, on a joint request. In Luxembourg, the employer declares telework to the CCSS as a monthly average percentage and must declare again when the situation changes. In Switzerland, for the framework agreement, the employer requests the A1 certificate through the ALPS platform.

**Data protection.** Under the GDPR, personal data must be "adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary" for the purpose (data minimisation). For these rules, what matters is the type of day worked, not a precise location.

## 2. What to record, and why

| Record | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Day type for each workday: at the employer's site, telework in the country of residence, work trip or mission elsewhere | Each rule counts different day types; Swiss rules count missions separately from telework, up to 10 days |
| Country of each mission (country of residence or a third country) | Swiss rules count missions in the country of residence before missions in a third country |
| Part-day work outside the employer's country | Luxembourg tax counts any part of a day as a whole day |
| Training days outside the employer's country | Count toward the Luxembourg 34 days |
| Non-working days: leave, sickness, rest days, public holidays not worked | Excluded from the Luxembourg 34-day count; sickness is still working time for the Luxembourg social security percentage |
| On-call periods with an actual intervention | Count as telework under Swiss rules only if the employee actually intervened |
| The counting unit you use (days, half days or hours) | Geneva lets the employer choose, but one method must apply to all staff |
| Contract working time, start and end dates | Part-time and part-year work reduce the Luxembourg 34 days (rounded down) and the Swiss 10 mission days (rounded up) |
| Changes of residence during the year | Geneva needs a rate only for the period of residence in France; the CCSS needs a new declaration |
| A1 certificates and their end dates | A framework agreement A1 lasts up to 3 years; on expiry the employer must apply again |
| Supporting evidence of presence (timesheets, mission orders, travel documents) | Listed by the tax authorities as proof for the Luxembourg threshold |

The official sources checked for this page do not set a specific retention period for these day records. Ask your adviser how long to keep them.

## 3. Four ways to track, compared

This comparison is practical guidance, not a legal assessment.

| Approach | Works well when | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Spreadsheet** | Few employees and one border route; you need something today at no cost | Manual entry and formulas that break; one person often owns the file; hard to prove who changed what and when; per-route rules (whole-day fractions, mission order, part-time proration) must be built and maintained by hand |
| **HR or time system** | You already record attendance and absences there; payroll and HR data stay in one place | Check whether it can record the country of each workday and missions separately from telework, and whether it applies each route's counting rule |
| **Dedicated tracker** | You have staff on several border routes and want separate tax and social security counters per employee | Another tool to adopt, and often to pay for; check that the rules it applies match your routes, that it uses your counting unit, and how it handles personal data and exports |
| **Employee self-declaration** (a periodic form or year-end statement) | Very few days outside the employer's country, or as a cross-check on other records | Relies on memory; a single year-end figure is hard to reconcile with a rule that asks for days actually worked; in Luxembourg, a log alone does not prove where someone was |

Whatever you choose, test it against these questions:

1. Can it separate telework in the country of residence from missions, and missions in the country of residence from missions in a third country?
2. Does it apply your chosen counting unit (whole days, half days or hours) the same way for every employee?
3. Can it treat any part of a day outside Luxembourg as a full day for Luxembourg tax?
4. Does it keep tax counters and social security percentages apart?
5. Can it handle part-time, part-year and a change of residence?
6. Does it collect only what the rules need, without device location?
7. Can you export per-employee records for the annual rate, CCSS declarations or an audit?

## 4. A simple routine that works with any method

1. **Each week:** employees confirm their day types for the week (site, telework, mission, non-working).
2. **Each month:** HR checks each employee against the thresholds that apply to their route, and flags anyone approaching 40% (Switzerland), 34 days (Luxembourg tax) or a change of social security band (25% and 50%).
3. **On any change** (move, new telework pattern, new activity): update the social security declaration or A1.
4. **At year end:** calculate and report each Swiss rate, including 0%, and file the CCSS declaration renewals that are due.
5. **Keep the evidence** together with the day records.

## 5. Where a dedicated tracker fits

A dedicated tracker is one option among these four. BorderDay, for example, lets employees log a day type for each date without GPS or device location, and gives HR separate tax and social security counters per employee for the border routes it covers, with a CSV export. Like any tool, it is a working record: it does not replace the official rate, attestation or A1 filings, and the employer's own figures prevail where its method differs.
